\begin{abstract}
Games serve as valuable testbeds for developing and evaluating algorithms and machine learning models, offering controlled environments in which their capabilities can be demonstrated and benchmarked. In 2024, Freinberger et al.\ showed that a hybrid model combining a parametrised quantum circuit with classical encoding and post-processing layers can solve Atari Pong and reach scores comparable to a classical reference on Breakout \cite{freinberger_quantum_2024}. Building upon this work, we extend the application of quantum machine learning to a considerably more complex game: we train a Quantum Neural Network (QNN) to complete the original DOOM level E1M1 from screen pixels, which demands perception, navigation, door handling and combat. Our agent is a classical CNN that compresses four stacked frames into 12 angles driving a 12-qubit, two-layer data-re-uploading circuit with 74 trainable parameters, whose Pauli-$Z$ expectations give the action logits. We compare it with a classical MLP head and a linear readout on an identical encoder. Two smaller ViZDoom scenarios first confirmed that the circuit can be trained end to end behind a CNN (96--99\% and 98\% success). On E1M1, reinforcement learning (PPO) alone never found the route in three attempts, two run to 10M decisions. Students that imitate a scripted teacher with access to private game state, using DAgger, exit E1M1 on 70--77\% of 100 held-out seeds (circuit: 73\% greedy, 70\% sampled; classical MLP 72\%/77\%; linear 77\%/72\%; teacher 84\%). Perturbation tests show these students rely on level-specific cues and the on-screen HUD. We find no evidence that the circuit contributes anything beyond a small linear readout.

\section{Application Problem and Motivation (15\% Weight)}
\label{sec:problem_motivation}

\subsection{Background and Domain Context}
Recent research has demonstrated the potential of hybrid quantum-classical reinforcement learning models in game-playing tasks, including Atari games such as Pong and Breakout \cite{freinberger_quantum_2024}. Building on these findings, this project investigates the application of Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs) to a more complex and dynamic environment, the first-person shooter Doom through the ViZDoom platform \cite{kempka_vizdoom_2016}, with the first level of the original game, E1M1 (Hangar), as the benchmark.

The target problem falls under reinforcement learning (RL), specifically visual, sequential decision-making under partial observability. E1M1 requires an agent to interpret high-dimensional visual observations, follow a route of about 5,000 map units through rooms, open doors with a USE action, avoid damaging floors, fight six monsters and finally press an exit switch. These challenges are relevant to real-world applications such as autonomous robotics, self-driving vehicles, and intelligent control systems, where agents must continuously process sensory information and adapt their actions to uncertain environments.

This project explores whether QNNs, through their parameterised quantum circuits and quantum feature representations, can offer an alternative approach to learning effective decision-making policies. Parametrised circuits have been used as policies and Q-functions on small RL benchmarks \cite{chen_vqc_drl_2020,jerbi_pqp_2021,skolik_gym_2022}, and careful comparison against classical baselines is essential when judging such models \cite{bowles_benchmark_2024}. We aim to train a QNN-based agent to complete E1M1 from visual observations while evaluating its learning performance, sample efficiency, and computational requirements against classical baselines. The objective is not to assume a quantum advantage, but to investigate whether QNN-based architectures offer practical benefits or introduce additional limitations in complex visual reinforcement learning tasks.

Before attempting E1M1 we checked feasibility on two smaller ViZDoom scenarios, a maze (My Way Home) and a combat corridor (Deadly Corridor); these pilots are summarised in Section~\ref{sec:pilots}. The rest of the report concerns E1M1.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

E1M1 is modeled as a partially
observable Markov decision process (POMDP)
$\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\Omega,O,\gamma)$
with $A=|\mathcal{A}|=7$ discrete actions (forward, attack, strafe left, strafe right, turn left, turn right, use).
The objective is to learn a policy that maximizes
expected cumulative reward, and completes the level, from visual observations.
We compare quantum and classical policy architectures
under a common training framework.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input:}
    The environment produces RGB frames of resolution
    $160\times120$ with the heads-up display (HUD) visible, which are downsampled to $80\times60$.
    Four consecutive frames are stacked to form
    $o_t\in\mathbb{R}^{12\times60\times80}$.
    A shared CNN encoder $f_{\eta}$ (three convolutional layers in the style of \cite{mnih_dqn_2015}) extracts a
    256-dimensional feature representation, followed
    by a 12-dimensional bottleneck:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{z}_t =
        \pi\tanh(W_b f_{\eta}(o_t)+\mathbf{b}_b)
        \in[-\pi,\pi]^{12}.
    \end{equation}
    The bottleneck representation is provided to one
    of three actor--critic architectures:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Classical MLP: nonlinear actor and critic (hidden width 32).
        \item Linear: a single affine map giving the policy logits and the value.
        \item Quantum: a 12-qubit variational actor--critic
        with two data-re-uploading layers; the action logits and the value
        estimate are both Pauli-$Z$ expectation values of the circuit (there is no classical critic).
    \end{enumerate}
    Each actor outputs logits over the $A$ discrete actions, defining
    $\pi(a_t|\mathbf{z}_t)$ through softmax.
    Position, depth, object labels and the kill counter are read from the engine only to compute
    the reward, auxiliary training targets and the teacher's labels; they are never inputs to the policy.

    \item \textbf{Objective / Cost Metric:}
    The reinforcement learning objective maximizes
    expected discounted return:
    \begin{equation}
        J=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
        \left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t\right].
    \end{equation}
    We first optimize all architectures with PPO \cite{schulman_ppo_2017}.
    The clipped policy loss is
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{policy}} =
        -\mathbb{E}_t\left[
        \min\left(
        \rho_t\hat{A}_t,
        \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)
        \hat{A}_t\right)\right],
    \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation}
        \rho_t =
        \frac{\pi(a_t|\mathbf{z}_t)}
        {\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t|\mathbf{z}_t)}
    \end{equation}
    and $\hat{A}_t$ is the advantage estimated using
    Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) \cite{schulman_gae_2016}:
    \begin{equation}
        \delta_t=r_t+\gamma(1-d_t)V(\mathbf{z}_{t+1})
        -V(\mathbf{z}_t),
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{A}_t=\delta_t+
        \gamma\lambda(1-d_t)\hat{A}_{t+1},
    \end{equation}
    where $d_t$ indicates episode termination.
    Advantages are normalized within each minibatch.

    The critic (the MLP critic, the linear value readout, or the circuit's value qubit) minimizes the value loss:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{value}}=
        \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_t
        \left[(V(\mathbf{z}_t)-\hat{G}_t)^2\right],
    \end{equation}
    with $\hat{G}_t=\hat{A}_t+V_{\mathrm{old}}
    (\mathbf{z}_t)$.
    The complete PPO training objective is
    \begin{equation}
        \boxed{
        \mathcal{L}=
        \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{policy}}
        +c_v\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{value}}
        -c_e\mathcal{H}(\pi)
        +c_a\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}
        }
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}$ comprises
    auxiliary losses used to train the shared visual representation: mean-squared error on per-sector depth,
    binary cross-entropy on whether a monster is visible in each of eight screen sectors and whether a door
    or the exit switch is directly ahead, and (in some runs) mean-squared error on the direction and progress along the route.
    They are used only during training \cite{mirowski_navigate_2017,lample_fps_2017}.

    \textbf{Imitation objective.} PPO from scratch did not learn E1M1 (Section~\ref{sec:ppo_attempts}),
    so the policies that clear the level are trained by DAgger imitation \cite{ross_dagger_2011} of a scripted teacher
    $\pi_T$ that reads private game state. With teacher action $a^\star_t=\pi_T(s_t)$ on the states $o_t$ visited by
    the current student, the loss is
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC}}=-\mathbb{E}_{o_t\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[w_{a^\star_t}\log\pi(a^\star_t|\mathbf{z}_t)\right]
        +c_a\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}},\qquad w_a\propto f_a^{-0.75},
    \end{equation}
    where $f_a$ is the frequency of action $a$ in the aggregated dataset $\mathcal{D}$, so that rare actions such
    as USE are not ignored. After each round the student drives, the teacher labels the visited states,
    and they are added to $\mathcal{D}$.

    \item \textbf{Reward:}
    The environment reward is
    \begin{equation}
        r_t=-0.01+100\,\mathbf{1}[\text{exit}]-30\,\mathbf{1}[\text{death}]-0.05\,\Delta\mathrm{HP}^{-}_t+2\,\Delta\mathrm{kills}_t
        +30\big(\gamma\Phi(s_{t+1})-\Phi(s_t)\big),
    \end{equation}
    with the potential-based progress term $\Phi(s)=\max(0,1-c(s)/c_0)$ and $\Phi=0$ at episode end.
    Here $c(s)$ is the walking cost from the player's position to the exit, taken from a path-planner field, since the
    route winds through rooms and doors and straight-line distance would reward walking into walls; $c_0=318.6$ is the cost at the start.
    The position and the field are used only inside the reward.

    \item \textbf{Scope and Constraints:}
    Experiments use E1M1 at skill 3 from the real start, with 6 monsters, three doors and a limit of 1,575 decisions per episode (3 minutes of game time; a decision is one policy action held for 4 game tics).
    All three architectures share the same CNN
    structure, 12-dimensional bottleneck, and training
    procedure. The quantum model uses
    12 simulated qubits and two data-re-uploading
    layers implemented in PennyLane, while the
    classical components use PyTorch.

    Classical simulation limits quantum circuit
    complexity and training throughput. Performance
    is assessed using the exit (completion) rate, route
    progress, kills and training cost on held-out
    environment seeds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Quantum Method and Technical Correctness (25\% Weight)}
\label{sec:quantum_method}

\subsection{Quantum Algorithm / Model Architecture}

We use a variational quantum circuit as the policy and value readout of the agent. It combines angle encoding, data re-uploading \cite{perez_salinas_reuploading_2020} and an ansatz in the hardware-efficient style, namely single-qubit rotations with a CNOT ring \cite{kandala_hea_2017}. The circuit has $n=12$ qubits and $L=2$ layers, reads the bottleneck $\mathbf{z}\in[-\pi,\pi]^{12}$ and has $74$ trainable parameters.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{State Preparation / Feature Map:} Each bottleneck value $z_i$ is encoded as the angle of an $R_Y$ rotation on its own qubit, scaled by a learned factor $s_{\ell,i}$ (initialised to 1), starting from $|0\rangle^{\otimes12}$. The same $\mathbf{z}$ is re-uploaded in every layer, with separate scales per layer, so the first-layer feature map $U_{\Phi}(\mathbf{z})=\bigotimes_i R_Y(s_{1,i}z_i)$ gives the product state $\bigotimes_i\big(\cos\tfrac{s_{1,i}z_i}{2}|0\rangle+\sin\tfrac{s_{1,i}z_i}{2}|1\rangle\big)$.
    \item \textbf{Parameterized Quantum Circuit (Ansatz):} Layer $\ell\in\{1,2\}$ applies a trainable single-qubit block followed by a ring of CNOT gates,
    \begin{align}
        V_\ell(\mathbf{z}) &= \bigotimes_{i=0}^{11} R_X(\beta_{\ell,i})\,R_Z(\alpha_{\ell,i})\,R_Y(s_{\ell,i}z_i),\\
        E &= \mathrm{CNOT}_{11\to0}\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{10\to11}\cdots\mathrm{CNOT}_{1\to2}\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{0\to1},\\
        |\psi(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle &= E\,V_2(\mathbf{z})\,E\,V_1(\mathbf{z})\,|0\rangle^{\otimes12}.
    \end{align}
    The trainable parameters are $\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\alpha,\beta,s\}$ (each $2\times12$, so $48$ angles and $24$ scales) and two scalar output gains $g_\pi,g_v$, giving $74$ in total. Angles are initialised as $0.05\,\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, with $g_\pi=2$ and $g_v=5$.
    \item \textbf{Measurement Observable:} Single-qubit Pauli-$Z$ observables $\hat M_k=Z_k$ are measured on the first $A+1=8$ qubits:
    \begin{equation}
        \ell_k=g_\pi\langle\psi|Z_k|\psi\rangle\quad(k=0,\dots,6),\qquad V=g_v\langle\psi|Z_7|\psi\rangle .
    \end{equation}
    The $\ell_k$ are the softmax logits and $V$ is the value estimate; qubits 8--11 are not measured but influence the outputs through the entanglement. All observables commute, so one circuit execution in the computational basis provides all of them. There is no learned classical layer after the circuit; the gains only set the output scale. Under imitation only the logits are trained, so the value gain stays at its initial value.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Theoretical Correctness and Complexity}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gradients.} Every parametrised gate has the form $R_P(\varphi)=e^{-i\varphi P/2}$ with $P\in\{X,Y,Z\}$, so the parameter-shift rule is exact:
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\partial \langle \hat{M} \rangle}{\partial \theta_j} = \frac{\langle \hat{M} \rangle_{\theta_j + \frac{\pi}{2}} - \langle \hat{M} \rangle_{\theta_j - \frac{\pi}{2}}}{2}.
    \end{equation}
    For an encoded input gate $R_Y(\varphi_{\ell,i})$ with $\varphi_{\ell,i}=s_{\ell,i}z_i$, the chain rule gives $\partial/\partial s_{\ell,i}=z_i\,\partial/\partial\varphi_{\ell,i}$ and $\partial/\partial z_i=\sum_\ell s_{\ell,i}\,\partial/\partial\varphi_{\ell,i}$, so the same shifted circuits also give the gradient passed back into the CNN encoder.
    In our experiments the circuit is simulated exactly and differentiated by reverse-mode automatic differentiation (backpropagation) in PyTorch, which gives the same gradients; parameter-shift would be needed on hardware.
    \item \textbf{Expressivity.} With the scales fixed, $L$ re-uploading layers make each output a Fourier series of degree at most $L$ in each input $z_i$ \cite{schuld_expressive_2021}, so with $L=2$ the circuit represents only low-frequency functions of the 12 bottleneck values. The outputs lie in $[-1,1]$ before the gains; the logit gain grows from $2$ to $16.7$ in our E1M1 circuit student, since confident choices need large logits.
    \item \textbf{Resources} (Table~\ref{tab:resources}). The qubit count equals the bottleneck width, and $A+1=8$ qubits are measured. The CNOT ring is a serial chain, so its depth is 12 per layer. Gradients by parameter shift need $2\times72=144$ circuit evaluations per sample: $72$ rotation gates carry the $48$ angle and $24$ scale parameters, and the two output gains need none.
    \item \textbf{Trainability.} The circuit is shallow ($L=2$) and acts on 12 qubits, so the exponentially vanishing gradients of deep random circuits \cite{mcclean_barren_2018} are not expected; we did not measure gradient variances.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation and Experimental Design (25\% Weight)}
\label{sec:implementation_design}

\subsection{Software Environment and Simulation Setup}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Software Stack:}
    The implementation is developed in Python 3.12 using PyTorch 2.14.1 for
    classical neural network components, PennyLane 0.45.1 \cite{bergholm_pennylane_2018} for quantum
    circuit simulation, and ViZDoom 1.3.2 with Gymnasium 1.4.0 for the environment. PennyLane integrates with PyTorch to enable
    end-to-end training of the hybrid quantum-classical neural network. Training uses a Slurm GPU cluster (NVIDIA V100); one ViZDoom process
    per environment (8 in parallel) runs on the CPU cores.

    \item \textbf{Backend Configuration:}
    Quantum circuits are simulated using PennyLane's
    \texttt{default.qubit} simulator with the PyTorch interface and \texttt{backprop} differentiation, assuming ideal,
    noiseless quantum operations. Exact statevector simulation
    is used during training and evaluation, without finite-shot sampling
    or hardware noise models.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The E1M1 Environment, Teacher and Training Protocol}
\textbf{Environment.} E1M1 is read from the shareware \texttt{doom1.wad}. A route planner (Dijkstra on a 16-unit grid, respecting wall clearance, step height and nukage floors, with doors passable) gives a 22-waypoint route of about 4,972 units with doors at waypoints 5, 17 and 20, and a reverse Dijkstra from the exit gives the walking-cost field behind the progress reward (12,976 reachable cells; cost 318.6 at the start, falling steadily along all waypoints). At skill 3 the level has four zombiemen and two imps. A door that is already opening closes again if USE is pressed, so the agent must press USE once and then walk. For training only, an episode can start at a route waypoint (never a door waypoint) facing the route within $\pm45^{\circ}$, which gives practice on every segment; evaluation always starts at the real start.

\textbf{Scripted teacher.} The teacher reads private state: position and heading, the walking-cost field (for the route direction) and door and switch geometry (a use ray). Monster positions come from the labels buffer, as a player would see them. Each decision it (1) fires if a monster at least 12 pixels tall is near the screen centre (every decision if it is 30 pixels or taller, otherwise every other one), (2) presses USE once when a door or the exit switch is within 40 units ahead, with a 25-decision cooldown, or (3) turns toward the route and walks, turning in place if it is stuck. Two earlier versions died in nearly every episode: one turned to chase every visible monster, the other stood still to fire at an imp. The final teacher exits on 84 of 100 held-out seeds at skill 3 (29 of 30 at skill 1).

\textbf{DAgger protocol.} Round 0 trains on 200K teacher-driven decisions; each of 8 further rounds lets the student drive (teacher mix 0.3, then 0) for 60K decisions, labels every visited state with the teacher's action, and retrains on all data collected so far (about 680K states at the end). Episodes start at the real start (25\%) or a random non-door waypoint, and are cut at 600 decisions.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Baselines}
Each experiment (configuration in Table~\ref{tab:experimental_setup}) compares three policy heads on the same encoder and bottleneck: the 12-qubit circuit (74 parameters), a classical MLP actor--critic (1,096 parameters; the main classical baseline) and a linear readout (104 parameters; a smaller classical control). Trivial baselines are random actions and always walking forward. A scripted teacher that reads private state provides an upper reference. The ablations are behaviour cloning on teacher demonstrations alone (no DAgger rounds), PPO without any teacher, and two circuit ablations: the CNOT ring removed, and the circuit's rotation angles frozen at random values.

We use completion as the main metric: the fraction of episodes in which the agent presses the exit switch while alive, on 100 held-out spawn seeds (20000--20099) not used in training or selection, with greedy action selection and with actions sampled from the policy. Best checkpoints are chosen on 32 separate seeds (10000--10031). As secondary metrics we report deaths, kills and route progress (which enter the training reward), together with wall-clock time.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Codebase Architecture}
The repository is organised around the screen-only pipeline. For E1M1, \texttt{e1m1\_map.py}, \texttt{e1m1\_planner.py} and \texttt{e1m1\_distance.py} read the level, plan the route and build the walking-cost field; \texttt{pixel\_e1m1\_env.py} wraps ViZDoom (observations, rewards, auxiliary targets, waypoint starts); \texttt{pixel\_nav\_models.py} defines the shared encoder and the three heads; \texttt{e1m1\_quantum\_model.py} is the circuit; \texttt{e1m1\_teacher.py} and \texttt{train\_e1m1\_imitation.py} implement the scripted teacher and DAgger; \texttt{train\_pixel\_nav.py} runs PPO, checkpoint selection and evaluation; and the \texttt{evaluate\_e1m1\_*.py} and \texttt{record\_gameplay.py} scripts produce the analyses and gameplay videos. The two pilot scenarios use \texttt{pixel\_nav\_env.py} and \texttt{pixel\_corridor\_env.py} with the same trainer. Every experiment has a Slurm script (\texttt{run\_*.sh}); results are written as JSON under \texttt{pixel\_e1m1\_runs/}, \texttt{pixel\_nav\_runs/} and \texttt{pixel\_corridor\_runs/}. The pinned environment is in \texttt{requirements.txt} (PyTorch from the CUDA 12.6 wheel index), and the \texttt{README.md} gives the commands and a dated log of all runs, including negative results. All seeds are fixed, and training, selection and held-out evaluation seeds are disjoint. The repository has no automated test suite; the \texttt{diagnose\_*.py} and smoke-test scripts check the environments.

A screen-only network clears E1M1 on 48--58\% of unseen seeds from teacher demonstrations alone (63--78\% sampled), and DAgger raises greedy play to 72--77\% at the same 680K-state budget, a gap of about two standard errors for the classical head while sampled play is unchanged (77 against 78). A first version of the teacher pressed USE from up to 72 units away and then walked on while the door opened, so a closed door in view was labelled USE only about one time in four; its greedy student never pressed USE and died at the doors at waypoints 17 and 20 (37 exits greedy against 83 sampled). \subsection{Analysis of Convergence, Scalability, and Circuit Depth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Convergence Behavior:} Under imitation all heads see the same data schedule, so the difference is in fit and cost. The circuit fits the teacher's labels less tightly (training accuracy 87\% against 91--92\% for the classical head, cross-entropy 0.31 against 0.24) and took 1 h 53 min against about 25 min. Selection exits on 32 seeds fluctuate by several episodes between rounds (classical 18--29, linear 22--29, circuit 17--26), so a single seed cannot rank rounds or heads. We did not diagnose barren plateaus.
    \item \textbf{Effect of Circuit Depth:} We used $L=2$ throughout and did not sweep the depth, so we have no depth--quality--runtime curve for E1M1. The pilots did show the circuit slower than classical heads in decisions as well as seconds: 1.79--2.56M against 0.77--1.28M decisions on the maze and 4.35M against 1.79--2.82M on the corridor, though one circuit seed cannot separate that from seed variation (Table~\ref{tab:pilots}).
    \item \textbf{Shot Noise Impact:} All results use exact expectation values, so shot noise was not measured. As an estimate only: an expectation from $N$ shots has standard deviation $\sqrt{(1-\langle Z\rangle^2)/N}\le 1/\sqrt{N}$, i.e.\ up to about $0.03$ for $N=1000$. The logit gain of $16.7$ learned by the E1M1 circuit student would turn that into logit noise of up to about $0.5$, which could change close action choices.
    \item \textbf{Does the circuit matter?} On E1M1 (Table~\ref{tab:e1m1}) and on both pilots the linear readout matches the circuit and the classical MLP; the CNN encoder does the perception and any small head can read it out. The circuit's 74 parameters are 0.015\% of the model. Two ablations of the E1M1 circuit student point the same way (Table~\ref{tab:e1m1}): removing the CNOT ring, so that each logit depends on one bottleneck value, gives 68 greedy and 67 sampled exits against 73 and 70, with the same training accuracy (87\%); and freezing all rotation angles at random values, with only the encoder and two output gains training, still gives 52 greedy and 66 sampled exits. Training the angles adds about 16--21 greedy exits (roughly 3--4 standard errors) but nothing clear when sampling, so most of the policy comes from the CNN and the entanglement is not needed.
    \item \textbf{Do the students memorise the level?} E1M1 is one fixed level, so held-out seeds change only monster behaviour. Perturbation tests without retraining (Table~\ref{tab:robust}) show the students depend on level-specific cues: started at a random reachable position and heading, exits fall from about 75--82 to 25--30 while the teacher gets 92; blanking the HUD (health, ammo) takes every student to 0 exits, although the classical student tolerates noise and brightness changes. The students also fail on the pilot scenarios (6--10 greedy exits on the maze against 10 for random actions, and 0--1 on the corridor), and the pilot-trained models make no progress on E1M1. So the result reflects learning one level, not general skills.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Critical Limitations and NISQ Feasibility}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Where the learning happens.} The classical parts (encoder, bottleneck and auxiliary head) hold 99.985\% of the parameters, and a linear readout does as well as the circuit. Our results show a circuit can be trained end-to-end behind a CNN to clear E1M1, and they show no benefit of doing so.
    \item \textbf{A privileged teacher was needed.} PPO scored 0 exits in three attempts. The students that clear E1M1 learn from a hand-written teacher that reads position, route and door geometry, so they measure how well a screen-only network can copy an expert, not whether reward alone finds the behaviour.
    \item \textbf{Memorisation.} The students recognise places along one route, helped by the HUD, and are lost away from it; they do not transfer to other tasks.
    \item \textbf{Cost.} Simulation makes the circuit about ten times slower per update, and statevector memory doubles with every added qubit.
    \item \textbf{Statistical strength.} Every E1M1 number uses one training seed and 100 test seeds; the differences between the heads (70--77 exits) are within noise.
    \item \textbf{NISQ feasibility.} The circuit has 24 CNOTs in a serial chain (depth about 30) with a wrap-around gate that needs ring connectivity or SWAPs. At typical two-qubit error rates of 0.5--1\%, the CNOT errors alone would leave roughly 79--89\% of the ideal state fidelity ($0.99^{24}$ to $0.995^{24}$), before readout error and decoherence; this is a rough estimate, not a hardware measurement. Training on hardware would need 144 shifted circuits per sample per step, so the practical use is inference only, with one circuit execution per decision and enough shots to resolve the logits.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Directions}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We trained a hybrid quantum-classical agent, a CNN encoder feeding a 12-qubit data-re-uploading circuit, to complete the original DOOM level E1M1 from screen pixels. Reinforcement learning alone failed; imitation of a scripted teacher with access to private state, using DAgger, produced a circuit student that exits on 73\% of unseen seeds in greedy play (70\% sampled), as does a classical MLP head (72\%/77\%) and a 104-parameter linear readout (77\%/72\%). The circuit costs about ten times more wall-clock time, and we find no evidence of a quantum benefit: removing its entanglement changes the result by no more than the noise, and a circuit frozen at random angles still reaches 52--66 exits. The students memorise the level and its HUD and do not transfer to other tasks.

Next steps, in the order we would run them: (i) more seeds for every head, and a statistical comparison of exits; (ii) DAgger with random off-route starts and HUD dropout, to test whether the failures away from the route are a coverage problem; (iii) a depth sweep ($L=1,2,3,4$) and a brickwork entangler of depth two per layer, which is closer to a hardware-efficient ansatz, with a depolarising-noise and finite-shot simulation to estimate the degradation on NISQ devices, and zero-noise extrapolation as mitigation; (iv) training across several levels to test generalisation; and (v) a recurrent head (a classical GRU first, then a quantum recurrent cell such as QLSTM \cite{chen_qlstm_2020}) if memory proves to be the cause of the remaining door failures.
